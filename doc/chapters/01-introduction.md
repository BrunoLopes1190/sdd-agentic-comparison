# Introduction

## Problem

AI coding agents now carry out complete development tasks.
They analyse repositories, plan changes, modify several files and run tests.
Spec-Driven Development (SDD) proposes that the specification should be the central artefact of this work, giving the agent persistent and structured context.

In little more than a year, several approaches have emerged that implement SDD in different ways, such as GitHub Spec Kit [@githubSpecKit2025], OpenSpec, Kiro and the BMAD Method.
They differ in the artefacts they require, the phases of the process and the points of human review.

The problem addressed is the lack of a common characterisation of the existing SDD approaches and of a comparison of their results when applied to the same project.

### Perspectives

The problem looks different to each group it affects.

**Teams and practitioners.**
A team that wants to adopt SDD has no comparable evidence on which to base its choice.
A poor choice is paid for in agent usage, rework and dependence on one workflow.

**Research.**
The available comparisons are almost all grey literature.
The academic works found so far compare approaches on other dimensions or without measurement.
Macedo [-@macedoPromptProcessProcess2026] compares six frameworks from their documentation and measures neither cost nor quality.
Panda [-@pandaCitationDisciplineSpecDriven2026] runs three approaches, but compares output determinism and hallucination detection, not cost.

**SDD approach creators.**
Each approach is described by its creators, in its own terms and with its own claims of benefit.
Without an independent comparison on the same project, these claims cannot be checked against each other.

### Consequences of not solving the problem

Without comparable evidence, teams choose an approach by visibility or by marketing.
More structure is not free.
Gloaguen et al. [-@gloaguenEvaluatingAGENTSmdAre2026] found that context files generated by a model raised the cost of coding agents by about 20%, with no significant gain in success.
An SDD approach can likewise add cost without adding quality, and no study measures this across approaches.

### Wider impact and legal concerns

- **Financial cost.** Agents are paid for by subscription or by token. The process an approach imposes changes how much agent work a project needs, so the choice has a direct financial effect. This is why total cost is part of the research question.
- **Quality and security.** Code generated by agents must still meet quality and security requirements. An approach that lowers cost but lowers quality moves the cost to maintenance. Both are measured together for this reason.
- **Licences and terms of use.** Each approach, agent and model has its own licence and terms of use, which govern the use of the generated code. They are treated in the ethics chapter.

## Stakeholders

The stakeholders were identified, classified and analysed with the power × interest matrix from the course's project charter module.
Power is the ability to influence the project.
Interest is how much the outcome of the project affects the stakeholder.
Both are rated on three levels:

- **Power.** High: can approve, stop or change the project directly. Medium: changes the conditions of the project (resources, tools, terms of use) without deciding on it. Low: cannot affect it.
- **Interest.** High: directly affected by the outcome and follows the work. Medium: partly or occasionally affected. Low: not affected and does not follow the work.

The matrix has two sides, so a medium rating is placed on the low side.

Three ratings need a short justification.
Anthropic can change usage limits, models or terms while the experiment runs, but it does not decide on the project.
The SDD approach creators release new versions that may change how an approach behaves, so the versions used are pinned.
Practitioners benefit from the decision guide but do not follow the work, so their interest is medium.
