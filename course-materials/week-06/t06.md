T2: Data integrity and research misconduct

Fabrication, falsiﬁcation or plagiarism [19]:
      1830: The mathematician Charles Babbage, often called the “father of the
      computer”, wrote about dishonest data practices, including “fabricating, cooking,
      trimming, and fudging data”.
      1909: The physicist Robert Millikan did not report 33 of the 149 observations of the
      oil-drop experiments for which he later received the Nobel Prize – those he had
      marked as “fair” or “poor”.

IFP (ISEP)  Ethics in research  2026/2027  12 / 54
T2: Data integrity and research misconduct (2)

Cases of data fabrication that led to retracted papers [19]:

      1974: William Summerlin admitted to fabricating data by using a marker pen to
      make black patches on white mice.

      2002: Jan Hendrik Schön, a rising star in physics, was found to have fabricated and
      falsiﬁed data in dozens of papers. Many of his papers were retracted.

      2014: Haruko Obokata was found to have fabricated and falsiﬁed data in
      high-proﬁle stem-cell papers, which were retracted.

Other stem-cell papers under scrutiny, in 2008 and in 2024:
      ‘Manipulated’ stem-cell paper faces retraction (2008);
      Retraction note: Pluripotency of mesenchymal stem cells derived from adult marrow
      (2024).

T2: Data integrity and research misconduct (3)

      Reproducibility [19]:
             1989: The “cold fusion” announcement by Pons and Fleischmann collapsed when
             dozens of laboratories could not reproduce their results.
             2013: The NIH launched an initiative to promote rigour, transparency and
             reproducibility in research.

      Warnings that were ignored [19]:
             1986: Roger Boisjoly, an engineer at Morton Thiokol, warned that the O-ring seals of
             the Space Shuttle Challenger could fail if it was launched in cold weather. His warnings
             were overruled, and the shuttle broke apart shortly after lift-oﬀ, killing its seven crew
             members [17].

T2: Authorship, credit and peer review

Authorship and credit [19]:

      1909: Robert Millikan did not name his student, Harvey Fletcher, as an author,
      despite Fletcher’s important contributions to the oil-drop experiments.

      1953: Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray diﬀraction image of DNA (“Photo 51”) was crucial
      for verifying the model proposed by James Watson and Francis Crick. It was shown
      to them without her permission.

      1997: The ICMJE revised its guidelines: to be an author, one must make a
      substantial contribution to the research design or to data collection, analysis or
      interpretation, and write the paper or revise it critically.

T2: Authorship, credit and peer review (2)

Failures of peer review and of editorial control [19]:
      Hoaxes (1996): The physicist Alan Sokal submitted a paper ﬁlled with nonsense to
      a cultural studies journal to test its rigour. It was accepted and published.
      Predatory journals (2010): Jeﬀrey Beall published a list of “predatory journals”,
      which charge high publication fees but provide poor or non-existent peer review.
      Rushed publication (2020): During the COVID-19 pandemic, the rush to publish led
      to the dissemination of invalid results and to multiple retractions, such as those of
      the papers based on data from the company Surgisphere.

T2: Ethics of new and dual-use technology

      Scientist-led governance (1973–1975): Scientists met to discuss the risks of the new
      recombinant DNA technology and to establish biosafety protocols.

      Dual-use research (2014): Research whose results can be used both for good and to
      cause harm. The concerns over bioterrorism and gain-of-function research highlight
      the dilemma of publishing information that could be misused.

      Generative AI (2024): The use of generative AI in research has raised new ethical
      issues concerning accountability, rigour, reproducibility, honesty and the allocation of
      credit.

T2: Human subjects protection

      Nuremberg Code (1947): The ﬁrst international code of research ethics. It states
      that “the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential” [13].

      Oversight (1974): In the USA, the National Research Act, passed after hearings on
      the Tuskegee study and signed in 1974, authorised federal agencies to develop
      regulations for research with human subjects.

             These require institutions to form Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) to review and
             oversee research with human subjects.

      The Common Rule (1991): The basic regulatory framework adopted by US federal
      agencies for the protection of human subjects in research.

T3: Ethics = law?

Most societies also have legal rules that govern behaviour, but ethical norms tend to be
broader and more informal than laws. Although most societies use laws to enforce
widely accepted moral standards, and ethical and legal rules use similar concepts, ethics
and law are not the same. An action may be legal but unethical, or illegal but ethical.

Ethics Versus the Law: The Case of the Belfast Project
      The Belfast Project, at Boston College, recorded testimonies from several sides of
      the conﬂict in Northern Ireland in the late 20th century. The participants received a
      written guarantee that their testimonies would be kept secret until after their
      deaths – a guarantee that could not be upheld when some of the recordings were
      subpoenaed.
      “there is even uncertainty about whether research institutions will keep their
      promises when facing external pressure”
      “it is not possible to fully inform someone about the risks they would be taking if
      they participate in research”

T3: The bias problem in scientiﬁc research

      Bias in scientiﬁc research refers to systematic errors or deviations that occur due to
      the inﬂuence of factors other than the phenomenon under investigation.
      To minimise bias, it is essential to use rigorous research methods and to be
      transparent about all aspects of the research process.

                                                                                                                                                                From [10].

T3: Common types of bias

      Sampling bias: Is the sample representative of the population being studied?
      Research may be based on a limited or unrepresentative set of software systems,
      developers or environments, which can skew the ﬁndings. For example, if studies
      focus mostly on open-source projects, the results may not generalise well to
      closed-source enterprise environments.
      Conﬁrmation bias: Researchers may unconsciously favour results that conﬁrm their
      hypotheses, and overlook data that contradict their assumptions.

T3: Types of research misconduct

Fabrication: making up data or results.

Falsiﬁcation: manipulating research materials, equipment or processes, or changing
or omitting data, so that the research is not accurately represented.

Plagiarism: using the ideas, results or words of others without giving appropriate
credit.

Honest error and diﬀerences of opinion are not research misconduct [18].

                                                                                     Based on [4, 1].

T3: Fabrication vs falsiﬁcation

      Fabrication: “making up data or results and recording or reporting them” – nothing
      real lies behind the data.
      Falsiﬁcation: “manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or
      changing or omitting data or results” so that the research is not accurately
      represented – the research was real, but was altered.
The question to ask: was there anything real behind the data?

       Reporting results of tests that were never run – fabrication.
       Altered images of real experiments (Kim Park’s case in The Lab) – falsiﬁcation.
       Millikan: omitting observations would be falsiﬁcation, if intentional and misleading.
       Schön, Obokata: both [19].
       Summerlin painted the mice to show skin grafts that had not succeeded: fabrication or
       falsiﬁcation?

                                                                                                                                                  Deﬁnitions from [21].

      Informed consent: ensuring that participants, if any, are fully aware of the purpose

      Misuse of technology: avoiding the development of technologies that could cause
      harm, be misused or lead to unintended consequences.
      Transparency: presenting all the results (positive, negative or neutral) to maintain
      academic integrity.

T5: Ethics in your dissertation
T5: Generative AI in the dissertation

Recommendations for researchers [5]:
      You remain ultimately responsible for the scientiﬁc output. AI systems are neither
      authors nor co-authors.
      Use generative AI transparently: declare the tools used substantially – which tool,
      which version, for what and how it aﬀected the work – usually in the methods
      chapter.
      Check what it produces: generative AI may produce plausible-sounding but
      incorrect citations and summaries, and its answers reﬂect the biases of its training
      data.
      Privacy, conﬁdentiality and intellectual property: do not upload unpublished
      work, personal data or the data and code of a company to external AI tools without
      guarantees that they will not be reused.

Hiding the use of AI or automated tools is an unacceptable practice [20].

T5: Generative AI – what to agree with your supervisors

To discuss and agree with the supervisors, task by task, before using it:
      help with the literature review (searching, summarising, comparing papers)? The
      papers must still be read;
      help with writing, translation or revision of the text?
      generation or refactoring of code, tests or scripts? Who veriﬁes it, and under which
      licence?
      generation of results of experiments that were not carried out? Presented as results,
      this is fabrication [1]; synthetic data or simulations are acceptable only when
      agreed, clearly identiﬁed and described in the methods.

What should not even need discussing: ﬁctitious bibliographic references. In one study,
55% of the citations generated by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those generated by GPT-4 were
fabricated [22]. Every reference must exist and must have been read.

T5: Plagiarism in practice

Plagiarism is “using other people’s work or ideas without giving proper credit to the
original source” [20] – not only copying a paragraph. It also includes:

      using ideas, sentences or texts of others without citing them [8] – also when they are
      paraphrased or translated;
      reusing ﬁgures, tables or code without attribution [1] (or against their licence);
      reusing one’s own work without citation (“self”-plagiarism), which “may be
      unethical and may violate copyright laws” [1]; the P.PORTO code forbids presenting
      as original a work already presented or published, without saying so [8].

At ISEP, the dissertation must be original and the student declares that the text is of
their exclusive authorship, with the contributions of others referenced; failing to comply
is a disciplinary oﬀence [9].

T5: Personal data in the dissertation

Surveys, interviews, usage logs or user data are personal data [6, 2]:
      have a lawful basis – in research with participants, usually their informed consent –
      and tell them what is collected, why, for how long and who has access;
      collect only what is needed (data minimisation) and deﬁne for how long it is kept;
      pseudonymised data are still personal data; only anonymous data fall outside the
      GDPR;
      store the data securely, and never in personal accounts or external AI tools.

Sensitive data or vulnerable participants: check whether an ethics committee must assess
the work (T3).

T5: Dissertations in a company

      The work is supported by a collaboration protocol between the company and ISEP
      [9].
      Conﬁdentiality: conﬁdentiality, for up to three years, can be requested when the
      examinations are requested; it must be validated and justiﬁed by the jury [9].
      Intellectual property: who owns the code and the results? Agree it in writing at the
      start – the regulation does not settle it [9].
      What can be published: agree early what may be described in the dissertation and
      in papers, and what must stay out.
      Conﬂict of interest: a personal or ﬁnancial interest “likely to bias” judgement [1] –
      for example, evaluating a product that you develop for the company that pays you.
      Declare it.

T5: Research data

Data and code should be FAIR [23]:
      Findable: a persistent identiﬁer (e.g. a DOI) and metadata;
      Accessible: retrievable through a standard protocol, even when access is restricted;
      Interoperable: open, documented formats;
      Reusable: a licence, the provenance and enough documentation to reuse them.

Decide at the start where the data and code are kept and versioned, what can be
published (and under which licence), and what cannot – because of personal data or
conﬁdentiality. FAIR does not mean open: it means well managed [12].

Closing

Three things to carry forward:
      ethics underpins all the phases of research – from the idea to the dissemination of
      the results – and not only the moment of data collection;
      legal and ethical are diﬀerent questions: an action may be legal but unethical, or
      illegal but ethical;
      in the dissertation: no fabrication, falsiﬁcation or plagiarism – nor ﬁctitious
      references; the use of generative AI agreed with the supervisors and declared; and,
      when the work involves people, personal data or a company, the rules agreed before
      the work starts.
